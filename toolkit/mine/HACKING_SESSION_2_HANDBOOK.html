The 25-minute crash course

Use pencil and paper for the three short calculations below. After each one, open the answer and compare the intermediate values. The full chapters repeat the notation so you can jump straight into any of them.

Minutes 0–8 · ElGamal has three different secrets

Bob keeps a long-term private exponent x. Alice picks a fresh secret exponent k for one encryption. Each computes the same shared secret S by a different route. Alice sends a pair of numbers. Bob uses the first number and his private key to undo the multiplication in the second.

On paperAlice, the senderBob, the receiver
StartPublic prime p = 23, generator g = 5, Bob’s public value y = 8.He chose x = 6. Indeed, 5⁶ mod 23 = 8.
Encrypt m = 7Choose k = 3. First component c₁ = 5³ mod 23 = 10. Her shared secret is S = 8³ mod 23 = 6.Receives (10, 19). The second component is 7 × 6 mod 23 = 19.
DecryptOnly (c₁, c₂) goes on the wire.His shared secret is 10⁶ mod 23 = 6. The inverse of 6 is 4 because 6 × 4 = 24 ≡ 1. Hence 19 × 4 mod 23 = 7.
Try it · If Alice uses k = 5 for the same message and key, what changes?

Her new c₁ is 20, the shared secret is 16 and c₂ is 20. Bob still obtains 7. His private x and public y stay fixed across encryptions. A matching c₁ across records is worth checking under the same group and recipient key.

Minutes 8–17 · Verify a DSA signature with two moduli

Start with public parameters p = 23, q = 11, g = 4, public key y = 18, message representative z = 7 and signature (r, s) = (3, 4). Group powers use p. Exponents and signature arithmetic use q. You can verify the signature with public information alone.

  1. Find the inverse of s modulo q. Since 4 × 3 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11), w = 3.
  2. Compute two exponents. u₁ = 7 × 3 mod 11 = 10 and u₂ = 3 × 3 mod 11 = 9.
  3. Compute the two powers modulo 23. 4¹⁰ mod 23 = 6 and 18⁹ mod 23 = 12. Multiply to get 72 mod 23 = 3. Finally 3 mod 11 = 3 = r. The signature verifies.
Try it · Keep (3, 4), but change the message representative from 7 to 8. What will the verifier do?

Now u₁ = 2 and u₂ = 9. The product modulo 23 is 8, and 8 modulo 11 is 8. Since 8 differs from r = 3, this signature fails for the changed message.

In DSA1, read the JSON’s hash field before doing any calculation. It specifies the entire SHA-256 digest as a big-endian integer reduced modulo q. Standard DSA uses the leftmost required bits of the digest. These rules can give different numbers for the same bytes. Compare both rules on b"UA".

Minutes 17–25 · Who owns the public key?

Alice looks up “Bob” and obtains a valid public key. Encrypting under it answers which private key can decrypt? The directory must separately answer who controls that private key? Signing a directory entry helps only if Alice already trusts the verification key and the authority verified the name.

QuestionEvidence to look for
Who is the peer?An independently trusted identity-to-key binding, such as a validated certificate for the intended service.
Does this peer control the key now?A signature or authenticated response covering this session’s fresh challenge and context.
Could an earlier message still pass?A fresh challenge consumed once, a checked sequence number, or a timestamp plus a replay policy.
Can the peer perform this action?An authorization decision for the authenticated identity and requested operation.
Try it · Alice and a peer compute identical Diffie–Hellman secrets. Which claim still needs evidence?

The peer’s identity. A valid DH calculation gives matching key material to the two participants in that exchange. A trusted signature over the exchange can bind it to Bob. A copy of Bob’s certificate alone does not show that this peer owns the corresponding private key.

ElGamal encryption

Read the symbols before the ciphertext

SymbolOwner and lifetimeMeaning
p, gPublic group parametersp is prime. Multiplication wraps modulo p. g generates the group used by the scheme. The slides call the prime q and the generator α.
x, yBob chooses secret x and publishes yy = gˣ mod p. The slide names are XA and YA when A is the receiver.
kAlice’s secret, one encryptionA fresh exponent in the allowed nonzero exponent range. For a group of order t, choose from 1 through t − 1.
S (or K)Computed independently for one encryptionA group element, S = yᵏ mod p = c₁ˣ mod p. It masks the message. It differs in role and units from the exponent k.
m, c₁, c₂m is a group element. The pair is public.In the slide integer model use 1 ≤ m < p. A subgroup variant needs its specified message encoding. c₁ = gᵏ, c₂ = mS, with both results reduced modulo p.

Why Bob can undo the mask

y = gx mod p  ·  c₁ = gk mod p  ·  c₂ = m · yk mod p
S = c₁x mod p  ·  m = c₂ · S−1 mod p
  1. Alice raises Bob’s public y to her private k. Substituting y = gˣ gives (gˣ)ᵏ = gˣᵏ.
  2. Bob sees c₁ = gᵏ and raises it to x. He obtains (gᵏ)ˣ = gᵏˣ. Multiplication of the two exponents commutes, so their results match.
  3. Since the shared element is nonzero modulo a prime, it has an inverse. Multiplying c₂ = mS by that inverse cancels the mask.

To practice, keep p, g and x fixed. Change the message to another valid group element and choose a new k. Check both the shared-secret equality and the final message. Slide 65 labels XA as public in its question. The decryption exponent there is the private XA, as defined on slide 60.

Two different hardness questions

Solving a discrete log is sufficient to recover an exponent in this model. Standard confidentiality arguments for textbook ElGamal in a suitable group use the decisional Diffie–Hellman (DDH) assumption. Large integers alone give no general DDH guarantee.

Two public parameter checks you can do by hand

In the full group modulo 23, g = 5 has order 22. Exponents 3 and 25 produce the same first component because their difference is one full period. The integer exponents need not be identical outside the specified canonical range. In a different design that explicitly uses the order-11 subgroup generated by 2, membership rules change.

The membership check assumes known prime p, known prime q and validated domain parameters. Apply the rules of the selected group. Applying the order-11 rule to the earlier full-group example would reject valid full-group elements.

Sign a digest representative, then verify it

r = (gk mod p) mod q    s = k−1 · (z + x·r) mod q
w = s−1 mod q    u₁ = z·w mod q    u₂ = r·w mod q
v = ((gu₁ · yu₂) mod p) mod q    Accept if v = r after range checks.

Set p = 23, q = 11, g = 4, x = 3, k = 4 and z = 7. Then y = 18. The group power 4⁴ mod 23 is 3, so r = 3. The inverse of 4 modulo 11 is 3. Hence s = 3 × (7 + 3 × 3) mod 11 = 48 mod 11 = 4. The crash course shows every verification value.

Why verification works. Signing implies k·s ≡ z + x·r (mod q). The verifier’s group expression is g raised to u₁ + x·u₂ because y = gˣ. That exponent is (z + x·r)·s⁻¹ ≡ k (mod q). Powers of g repeat after q steps. The verifier therefore recomputes the same group element and the same r.

The second assertion changes one input at a time. It checks that this particular changed message fails. Tiny groups can produce collisions, so one negative test alone cannot validate an implementation. In a real format, parse and validate the domain, public key, signature encoding and exact signed bytes with the designated library.

Standard DSA and DSA1 hash the same bytes differently

For standard DSA, use the leftmost min(hash length, q bit length) bits. Here q has four bits, and the digest starts with hexadecimal 1c, so its first four bits represent 1. The DSA1 JSON instead calls for the entire digest modulo q. hexdigest() creates printable text. Hashing that text again changes the input.

RSA-PSS, classic ElGamal signatures and ECDSA

RSA signatures. In textbook arithmetic, signing raises a raw representative to d and verification raises the signature to e. Raw RSA’s algebra admits signature forgery. RSA-PSS hashes the message, encodes it with salt and a mask generation function, and checks the entire encoding. A verifier must use the agreed hash, MGF hash and salt policy. See the working RSA-PSS example.

Classic ElGamal signatures · textbook supplement. With generator g of the full group modulo prime p, choose k coprime to p − 1. Compute r = gᵏ mod p and s = k⁻¹(z − x·r) mod (p − 1). The public verification equation is gᶻ ≡ yʳ · rˢ (mod p). The signing equation gives z ≡ x·r + k·s (mod p − 1), which explains that equality. Here the inverse uses p − 1. In DSA it uses q.

ECDSA · textbook supplement. The group operation is elliptic-curve point addition. With base point G of order n, private scalar x gives the public point Q = xG. Signing takes r from the horizontal coordinate of kG, reduced mod n, and s = k⁻¹(z + r·x) mod n. Verification forms u₁G + u₂Q and compares its reduced horizontal coordinate to r. Reject invalid parameters, points, ranges and the point at infinity where appropriate. A curve point and a scalar are different data types.

Course notation and current use

DSA is taught here to read course exercises and historical signatures. FIPS 186-5 allows verification of legacy DSA signatures generated before its implementation date. For new signatures in that standard, the approved families are RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA.

A numerical reason that repeated r is a clue

In the tiny DSA group p = 23, q = 11, g = 4, nonce 6 gives group element 2 and nonce 9 gives group element 13. Both give r = 2 after reducing modulo 11. Thus two distinct nonces can give the same r. Repetition in distinct messages under a real signer still deserves investigation; this tiny group makes the collision easy to see.

Key distribution and mutual trust

Diffie–Hellman by hand

Public parameters are a prime p and generator g. Alice chooses secret a and sends A = gᵃ mod p. Bob chooses secret b and sends B = gᵇ mod p. Alice computes Bᵃ mod p. Bob computes Aᵇ mod p. These are both gᵃᵇ mod p.

Man-in-the-middle, key substitution and authenticated exchange

Key substitution is a failure of the name-to-key binding. Impersonation is acceptance of a peer under another identity. A passive eavesdropper only sees the exchange. An active intermediary can affect it when that binding is missing. A key’s length, a successful encryption, or a hash fingerprint copied from the same untrusted directory settles none of these identity questions.

Authenticated key exchange. Verify a signature or a MAC under an already trusted credential over both shares, the roles, peer identities as required by the protocol, and the session context. Check a key-confirmation message when the protocol requires it. For example, TLS 1.3 certificate authentication binds a handshake transcript with CertificateVerify, and Finished authenticates the transcript with a handshake-derived key. One-way server authentication and mutual authentication make different identity claims.

Read an X.509 chain from the trust anchor

A certificate authority (CA) vouches for a binding. A leaf certificate carries a public key, subject information, validity period, serial number and extensions. A CA signature protects the certified fields. An existing trusted root or public key supplies the starting point. A self-signature alone supplies no independent trust.

  1. Build a path from the leaf through appropriate intermediates to a configured trust anchor. Verify issuer signatures, validity periods, permitted CA roles and path length, applicable name constraints, key usage and extended key usage.
  2. For a TLS service, match the intended service identity against the certificate’s appropriate subjectAltName under the service’s matching rules. A valid chain for the wrong service still fails this check.
  3. Apply the relying party’s revocation policy, for example certificate revocation lists (CRLs) or OCSP status. Expiry and revocation answer different questions.
  4. During the handshake, verify proof that the peer controls the certified private key for this session. Knowing a public certificate is easy for anyone.

Symmetric distribution, KDCs and tickets

A key distribution center (KDC) shares a long-term master key with each participant and issues short-lived symmetric session keys. A CA signs public identity-to-key bindings. With direct pairwise provisioning, N parties can require N(N−1)/2 pair keys. With one KDC, N master relationships suffice within a realm. For 10 parties that is 45 versus 10. The KDC is trusted with session keys and must be protected.

The course also covers physical delivery, a trusted courier, a new session key protected by an existing shared key, and hierarchical KDCs for multiple domains. For asymmetric key transport, first authenticate the recipient’s public key, then protect a newly generated symmetric session key for that recipient. In key agreement such as DH, both sides contribute to the result.

The symmetric Needham–Schroeder-style example on slides 17 and 52 distinguishes possession from freshness. A fresh challenge can show knowledge of the session key. If an old session key was compromised, accepting its old ticket lets knowledge of that older key count as current possession. Slides 55–56 add the responder’s nonce and a responder-chosen expiry time. A still-valid ticket can be reused with fresh challenges before expiry.

Certificate-based mutual authentication on slide 66. A trusted authority certifies peer keys and binds a session key to both identities and Alice’s fresh nonce. Hybrid encryption. Public-key transport or an authenticated key exchange establishes key material. A KDF derives purpose-specific keys when the protocol requires it. An AEAD cipher protects the bulk data and binds associated context. Sender authentication depends on the chosen exchange or an additional signature. For an example of hybrid building blocks, see RSA-OAEP and authenticated encryption.

Verify a directory binding and possession separately

This local example gives the verifier a trusted authority key in advance. The authority signs an enrollment record for Bob. Bob separately signs a fresh, context-bound challenge. The verifier checks both claims using different public keys. Assume the authority checked Bob’s identity at enrollment.

The authority key and Bob’s key serve different roles. A valid enrollment record can be copied by anyone. Only Bob’s key owner can produce the required new proof under the signature assumption. In a deployed system the record also needs a defined lifetime, rotation policy and canonical format, and the verifier must track challenge consumption.

RSA refresher

Build an RSA key, then check it

SymbolMeaningExample
p, q, nTwo distinct secret primes and their public product.p = 61, q = 53, n = 3233.

e, dPublic e must be coprime to λ. Choosing e coprime to φ and inverting it mod φ also works. d is the private exponent with e·d ≡ 1 mod λ.e = 17, d = 2753. Then 17 × 2753 = 46,801 = 15 × 3120 + 1.
m, cm is an encoded integer in 0 … n−1. Raw encryption c = mᵉ mod n. Raw decryption m = cᵈ mod n.m = 65 yields c = 2790. OAEP supplies a randomized encoding before the RSA operation.

Why the round trip succeeds. Modulo p, if m is zero then mᵉᵈ is also zero. Otherwise Fermat gives mp−1 ≡ 1. Since ed − 1 is a multiple of p − 1, mᵉᵈ ≡ m. The same argument holds modulo q. Two residues that agree modulo both primes agree modulo n by CRT. This proof includes messages divisible by one of the primes.

Efficiency. Modular exponentiation uses repeated squaring, with a number of modular multiplications on the order of the exponent’s bit length. Standard public exponent 65537 has few set bits. For a known private key, CRT can speed up private operations by working modulo the private primes. Implementations must also protect against timing and fault leakage.

OAEP encrypts and PSS signs

OAEP combines a random seed, a label hash and mask generation to encode a message before RSA. For modulus length k bytes and hash output h bytes, the maximum message length is k − 2h − 2 bytes. With 2048-bit RSA and SHA-256 this is 256 − 64 − 2 = 190 bytes. For large data, use a hybrid design. PSS encodes a message digest and salt for a signature. The verifier checks the specified encoding, parameters and signature length.

The public key suffices for verification and encryption. Only the respective private key signs or decrypts. Two valid OAEP ciphertexts can differ while decoding to identical bytes. In PyCryptodome, explicitly select the agreed hash and configure any nondefault label, MGF or PSS salt parameters at both ends.

Modular arithmetic without guesswork

Remainders, GCD and division

a ≡ b (mod n) means a and b have the same remainder on division by n, or equivalently n divides a − b. For positive n, Python’s a % n chooses a representative in 0 … n − 1. You may reduce after every addition or multiplication. Example: 20 × 15 mod 7 = 6 × 1 mod 7 = 6.

Modular division by a means multiplication by the integer u such that a·u ≡ 1 (mod n). An inverse exists exactly when gcd(a,n) = 1. For 3 modulo 91, notice 91 = 30 × 3 + 1. Rearranging gives 1 = 91 − 30 × 3. Thus −30 is an inverse; its least nonnegative representative is 61. Check 3 × 61 = 183 = 2 × 91 + 1.

pow(a, b, n) uses modular exponentiation throughout. A negative integer b first requires an inverse. Ordinary / produces a floating-point quotient, and // floors. Neither implements modular division. In Python, -(7 // 3) == -2 while -7 // 3 == -3. Parentheses matter.

Euclid uses gcd(a,b) = gcd(b, a mod b) until the remainder is zero. For 30 and 18 the remainders are 12, 6, 0. Extended Euclid also gives coefficients s and t such that s·a + t·b = gcd(a,b), called Bézout’s identity. E.g. −1 × 30 + 2 × 18 = 6. The coefficients are not unique. For coprime inputs, the coefficient of a is its inverse modulo b.

CRT and exact integer roots

The Chinese Remainder Theorem combines remainders for one unknown integer. Suppose it is 2 mod 3, 3 mod 5 and 2 mod 7. The smallest nonnegative solution is 23, and 23 + 105·t works for every integer t. With pairwise coprime moduli, the combined modulus N = 3 × 5 × 7 = 105. CRT returns the residue and its period. It does not say which integer in that class was intended.

Why it works for coprime moduli: N divided by any one modulus contains all the other factors. This makes each term zero in the other residue systems. An inverse rescales the term to one in its own system. Summing the scaled terms combines the desired residues. Directly check each remainder at the end.

An exact integer root exists when an integer power equals the input over ordinary integers. The floor cube root of 126 is 5 because 125 ≤ 126 < 216, yet 5³ differs from 126. isqrt returns the floor square root; square it to check exactness. Floating-point roots can silently round huge values. For non-coprime congruences, use solve_congruence for general systems; compatible periods are governed by the least common multiple.

Continued fractions by hand

Repeated division turns a rational number into a sequence of integer quotients. For 43/19: 43 = 2·19 + 5, 19 = 3·5 + 4, 5 = 1·4 + 1, 4 = 4·1. The coefficients are [2, 3, 1, 4]. The successive convergents are 2/1, 7/3, 9/4 and 43/19. They approximate the original ratio with small denominators. This is the number-theory concept behind the small-private-exponent topic in RSA.

Bytes, block widths, JSON, hexadecimal and Base64

Use json.load(file) for a file opened as UTF-8 and json.loads(text) for text you already hold. JSON numeric fields become Python integers. A large decimal string needs int(text, 10). A hex integer string needs base 16. For example, the characters 41 can also encode the single byte b"A". Decode according to the field definition.

A fixed-width encoding and minimal encoding answer different questions. Preserve the supplied block width and original length when rebuilding a sequence of blocks. Decode as UTF-8 only if the format says it contains UTF-8. Otherwise inspect raw.hex() or compare the bytes directly.

SHA-256 counter blocks and XOR

XOR compares corresponding bits and produces 1 exactly when the bits differ. For example, 0x41 XOR 0x03 = 0x42, and 0x42 XOR 0x03 = 0x41. A counter-derived stream concatenates blocks numbered 0, 1, … and truncates to the needed byte count. Hash inputs are bytes, so the seed representation, counter representation, separator, encoding and block order all matter.

This example deliberately defines its own four-byte counter. DSA1 describes a different text representation. A binary counter, decimal characters and hexadecimal characters can encode the same integer as different byte strings. XOR alone also gives no integrity guarantee. In a real application use a standard KDF and authenticated cipher suited to the protocol.

Authenticated encryption with a tag

Use a unique nonce per encryption under each GCM key. A recipient may use the original nonce to verify that record. Generate a fresh nonce when creating a new record. The tag covers associated data as well as ciphertext, and a failed tag must prevent plaintext release.

GET, POST and PUT with no network connection

GET reads a resource. POST asks a resource to process the enclosed data. PUT creates or replaces the state at the named resource according to the API contract. The example exercises the actual Requests functions against an in-memory mock and inspects the resulting prepared requests.

Modulus lookup

RSA group powers use n. ElGamal group powers use p. Classic ElGamal signature inverses use p−1. DSA group powers use p, while DSA signature inverses, u₁ and u₂ use q. A single wrong modulus can produce plausible numbers throughout.

Final attack-selection checklist

  1. Inventory the input. Record public parameters, keys, ciphertext pairs, signature pairs, exact message bytes and any trusted information. Separate observed facts from assumptions.
  2. Classify repeated values. Same RSA n, a proper common prime factor, same ElGamal c₁ and same signature r refer to different mathematical objects. Compare the relevant group and identity first.
  3. Establish the conditions. Small e or large e alone proves little. Distinct c₁ values do not demonstrate independence. A valid public key does not establish its owner. Write down the missing condition.
  4. Pick the modulus and encoding. Mark p, q, p−1, n or the group order next to every operation. Specify message encoding, hash convention, block width, byte order and counter representation.
  5. Test an independent property. Recompute a public equation, every CRT residue, a signature verification, or an authenticated identity check. Exercise a changed input and confirm the expected rejection where appropriate.
